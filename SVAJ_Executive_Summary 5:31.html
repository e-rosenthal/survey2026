The Central Argument

One Strategy, Two Cohorts

What the Survey Tells Us to Do

Make young families with children the primary beneficiaries of programmatic investment — because they are the generation who will grow SVAJ, raise the next generation of community members, and determine whether this shul thrives in 20 years. At the same time, reposition the legacy and senior membership not as passive service recipients, but as the volunteer, donor, and mentorship engine that makes investing in young families possible. The survey reveals exactly where these two cohorts need each other: seniors have the time young families don't have, and young families bring the energy and children that give seniors' continued involvement meaning. This is not a tradeoff. It is one strategy.

The survey didn't surface this framing — the data demanded it. Young families (under 45 with kids) cite childcare logistics as their #1 engagement barrier (51%). Senior members volunteer at low rates (2.13/5) despite having capacity, and rate "intergenerational connection" as a top-3 priority. The match is sitting unmade. A strategic plan that puts it on paper and assigns someone to execute it is the beginning of the flywheel.

What We Found

8 Things the Survey Made Clear

01

Youth programming is the most urgent operational problem — and it has three separate failure modes

Children's and youth programming scored 2.67/4.0 — the lowest of any domain, by a wide margin. But it's not one problem: parents of toddlers face timing mismatches (nap-time programs, post-bedtime events); parents of school-age kids (7–12) report unstructured groups with no curriculum and unsupervised children; parents of teens report nothing at all — their kids are actively choosing other shuls. Fix requires a dedicated full-time Youth Director — not a clergy person with a side portfolio.

2.67/4 satisfaction · Every demographic agrees · Competitors are already stepping up
02

Rav Ari is the institution — and that is a structural vulnerability

107 of 175 respondents (61%) cited the rabbi and clergy as their primary reason for joining — 2.5× the next factor. Multiple respondents explicitly name what happens if he leaves. The community has not yet demonstrated it can sustain its culture independent of any one individual. The work of institutionalizing his approach must begin now, not when it's urgent.

107 mentions · Most cited factor by 2.5× · Several respondents name succession risk explicitly
03

Financial sustainability is #1 — and the precondition for everything else

The community rated financial sustainability as the top strategic priority (3.92/5; 71% rated it Essential or Very Important). The physical facility scored 3.09/4.0 — second-lowest satisfaction. The front entrance stairs appear as a complaint in over a dozen responses. Without a capital campaign and professional fundraising capacity, the Youth Director hire, the facility upgrade, and every other priority remain aspirational.

3.92/5 — #1 of 17 priorities · 71% rated Essential/Very Important · Facility scores 3.09/4
04

The community is growing faster than its sense of belonging can keep up

17–19% of new members, young families, and people without children all independently cite "don't feel socially connected" as an engagement barrier. Multiple respondents describe the Kiddush as "overwhelming." Cliques are forming. The warmth that drew people in is not guaranteed to scale. Structural intimacy — grade cohorts, onboarding programs, assigned connectors for new members — must be built deliberately.

17–19% report social connection gap across all segments · "Too big to feel small" named repeatedly as top risk
05

Israel belongs in the mission statement — the community is already there

Connection to Israel rates 4.42/5 in values centrality — tied with davening and halachic commitment. The single most common mission statement change request, across all age groups and observance levels, is adding explicit Israel and Zionist language. The absence of Israel from the current statement is noticed and named as a gap by dozens of respondents. This is a near-consensus ask that requires careful language, not avoidance.

4.42/5 values centrality · Most-cited mission amendment · Consensus across seniors (3.73), young adults (3.61)
06

Adult learning is deeply wanted by the half of the congregation that doesn't have young kids at home

Seniors rate adult learning as their #1 strategic priority (4.14/5). Members without kids at home rate it at 4.14 vs. 3.26 for households with children — the sharpest demographic split in the dataset alongside youth programming. Yet only 3% of respondents attend shiurim weekly, and 32% rarely or never engage. The offering isn't reaching the cohort that wants it most.

4.14/5 for seniors and no-kids households · Only 3% attend weekly · 32% rarely/never engage
07

Women are carrying the operational load and flagging where it's breaking

Women rate nearly every strategic priority higher than men. The largest gender gaps: intergenerational connection (+0.59), planning/programming transparency (+0.39), member wellness (+0.37), chesed (+0.29), and leadership pipeline (+0.26). The planning transparency gap is diagnostic: women manage family schedules and are most affected by last-minute announcements and inconsistent programming times. Communication systems should be designed for them.

Women rate 15/17 priorities higher · Planning transparency gap +0.39 · 37% want expanded women's ritual roles
08

Seniors, singles, and families navigating challenges feel overlooked — and they're right

Older adults and seniors are the most commonly cited group that SVAJ could do better at including. The community's family-forward culture creates a parallel problem for those outside that structure: divorced members, widowed members, adults without children. Physical accessibility (stairs, elevator, acoustics) is a concrete and recurring complaint from the senior cohort specifically. The community that builds for its legacy members while investing in its youngest ones is the one that endures.

Seniors = #1 most-cited underserved group · Facility accessibility = universal complaint · Singles gap acknowledged by multiple respondents
The Three Segments

Who Needs What — and What We Should Ask of Them

🌱 Young Families · n≈63

Under 45, kids at home

  • Full-time Youth Director, immediately
  • Structured, age-segmented Shabbat groups
  • Teen spiritual track (minyan, Q&A, chavurah)
  • Programs timed for young children
  • Grade cohort social events
  • Childcare during adult programming

What they can give back: long-term membership, future leaders, dues revenue, energy

🏛️ Legacy & Senior Members · n≈67+

Age 55+, long-tenure

  • Serious adult learning — their #1 ask
  • Israel programming and Zionist identity
  • Physical accessibility (stairs, elevator)
  • Honored, meaningful volunteer roles
  • Intergenerational programming
  • Recognition as founders, not footnotes

What we should ask of them: madrichim, Shabbat hosts, new family mentors, major donors

🏗️ Middle Cohort · n≈54

Ages 40–54, Rav Ari era joiners

  • Teen programming — their kids are leaving
  • Financial investment in the building
  • Formal leadership opportunities
  • Less chaos, more planning transparency
  • Better sense of community belonging

Most available volunteers (2.43/5). Most anxious about under-investment. Next generation of board leadership.

"Not sure where I would fit in. I was very active when I first joined and miss that involvement." — Senior member, expressing desire to contribute, currently underutilized
"If I am going to commit to a committee or opportunity I want to be able to fully commit and with small kids it is too difficult to get more involved." — Young family member, explaining why she can't volunteer more

These two quotes are from the same survey. They describe a matching problem, not a resource problem.

What to Watch

Six Tensions That Must Be Actively Managed

These are not problems to solve — they are inherent to what SVAJ is. The goal is to name them, manage them openly, and prevent any one of them from becoming a crisis through drift or avoidance.

Growth vs. Intimacy

The warmth that drew people in is what scale puts most at risk. Must be designed for, not assumed.

Youth Investment vs. Adult Depth

Young families and older members want fundamentally different things. Both are right. Sequenced investment, not a single budget line.

Inclusion vs. Halachic Identity

Both "too left" and "too right" complaints coexist. SVAJ's big tent is an asset — until the rope snaps. The center must be stated, not just implied.

Clergy Dependency vs. Resilience

Rav Ari's culture must be institutionalized before succession is urgent. That work takes years and must start now.

Volunteer Capacity vs. Young Family Bandwidth

Young families want to give more but can't. Senior members have capacity but aren't channeled. The matching is the work.

Financial Ambition vs. Current Capacity

The community's stated priorities require more investment than current dues structure provides. Without intentional fundraising, every priority is in competition with every other.

Closing

"Right now, Skokie is looking to SVAJ as the voice of our Jewish community. It's ours to lose." The community knows what it needs. The goodwill, the talent, the growth momentum — all of it is there. What remains is the institutional will to invest intentionally, to ask the older generation to actively support the younger one, and to build the infrastructure that will make this community's culture outlast any single leader or era.
