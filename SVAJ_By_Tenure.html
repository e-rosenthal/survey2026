How Members Feel
Depends on When They Arrived

Breaking 175 survey respondents into five tenure cohorts reveals that SVAJ is experienced very differently depending on how long you've been here — and that the newest members have the most urgent and distinct set of asks. The oldest members carry the institution's memory and anxiety. The middle cohorts are quietly the most critical.

The Single Most Important Headline

The 7–10 year cohort (2015–2017, the early Rav Ari joiners) has the lowest composite satisfaction of any group (3.28) — lower than brand-new members, lower than legacy members. They've been here long enough to have strong opinions and high expectations, but not long enough to feel the ownership legacy members feel. They also have the sharpest youth programming complaints (2.45/4) and are the most financially anxious (4.13/5 priority). This is the cohort that will either become SVAJ's next generation of leaders — or quietly drift away.

Who They Are & What They Say

A Portrait of Each Cohort

Each cohort arrived during a distinct chapter of SVAJ's story. That shapes not just what they want, but how they frame problems, what they're afraid of, and what they believe SVAJ is.

10+ Years · Joined pre-2015 · n=40

The Founders and Keepers

Many were here before Rav Ari. They chose SVAJ for its hashkafic alignment (the top draw — more than the rabbi, unlike every other cohort). They carry the most institutional memory, have the deepest sense of ownership, and score the highest on composite satisfaction (3.49) and integration (4.22). But they're also the group most worried about what SVAJ is becoming — specifically anxious about the Kol Sasson relationship, space crowding, and the loss of the "vatikim" generation that defined the community's texture.

7–10 Years · Joined 2015–2017 · n=41

The Rav Ari Joiners — Most Critical, Most Invested

They came overwhelmingly because of the rabbi (32 mentions — the highest raw number of any cohort). They have the highest Shabbat attendance (4.24/5), highest mission statement alignment (4.07), and — paradoxically — the lowest composite satisfaction of any group (3.28). They've seen SVAJ at its best and are most acutely aware of the gap between aspiration and delivery. They are the most financially anxious (4.13/5) and have the most to say about youth programming, the building, and institutional direction. They are the strategic planning committee's most important audience.

5–7 Years · Joined 2018–2019 · n=24

The Pre-Pandemic Settlers — Quietly Peripheral

A smaller cohort that arrived just before the pandemic reshuffled community life. They have the highest mission statement alignment (4.25/5) — they feel most ideologically at home — but one of the lower integration scores (3.88). Their free-text responses are the most candid: they sense "different factions" at SVAJ, note that people "on the periphery" feel the cure is to leave. They have the lowest Shabbat attendance (3.87) of any group, suggesting they're connected but not fully embedded. They are SVAJ's quiet middle — neither anchors nor newcomers.

Highest mission alignment: 4.25 Lowest Shabbat freq: 3.87 Integration: 3.88 — low Top barrier: Schedule + childcare tied

2–5 Years · Joined 2020–2022 · n=42

The Pandemic-Era Wave — Family-Forward and Anxious About Scale

The largest single cohort. They arrived during COVID and post-COVID when SVAJ was already growing rapidly. They have the lowest Shabbat attendance of any group (3.67) — possibly reflecting pandemic habits that haven't fully re-normalized — but highest family engagement priority (3.84) and youth programming priority (4.11). They are the "too big to feel small" cohort: their top risk responses are about cliques, losing community feel, and financial under-investment. The Israel priority is notably high for this group (3.59) — they appear to want SVAJ to be explicitly Zionist.

Past 2 Years · Joined 2023+ · n=24

The Newest Members — The Most Demanding and the Most Hopeful

They have the highest Shabbat attendance (4.30 — more than any other cohort, including legacy members) and rate Israel programming priority highest of all (3.82). They also rate the welcoming score highest (4.21) — confirming SVAJ's on-ramp is working. But they have the highest rate of not knowing what's available (5 out of 24 cite it as a barrier), and their top family engagement priority (4.14) and planning transparency priority (4.14) signal that they arrived expecting more structure than they found. Critically, word-of-mouth from friends is now tied with the rabbi as the top draw — a sign of a maturing community reputation.

What the Barrier Shift Tells Us

  • Legacy members are hitting a ceiling — "already engaged" means SVAJ is successfully keeping them in, but there's no deeper tier to graduate to
  • The childcare problem peaks at 5–7 years — these families have settled in and have the most young kids at home right now
  • "Don't know what's available" is highest among newest members — a pure communications and onboarding failure, not a programming failure
  • "Don't feel connected" appears in every cohort at 6–7 mentions — it is persistent and tenure-independent, pointing to a structural belonging gap rather than a new-member issue
  • The 7–10 year cohort's #1 barrier is schedule — they're in peak career and parenting years; programming timing must serve them
A Special Case

Israel Priority — The Trend Is Unmistakable

Israel programming priority scores show the clearest directional trend of any item in the survey: it rises with recency of joining. The community is becoming more explicitly Zionist over time, not less.

Israel Programming Priority (1–5 scale)

The 5–7 year cohort (2018–2019) is the notable outlier at 3.13 — lowest of any group. This cohort arrived just before October 7 and the surge of explicit Zionist identity that followed. Their Israel scores on both priority and satisfaction are the lowest, suggesting a cohort that is more ambivalent or more cautious about politicizing the Israel question.

The newest members (3.82) — most of whom joined after October 7 — appear to have chosen SVAJ partly because of its Israel identity. One new member notes: "Spirit of inclusion and commitment to Israel are palpable in all the events." Another cites "Zionistic, welcoming" as their ideal future SVAJ. The post-October 7 community is arriving pre-sorted for Zionist alignment.

Strategic Implication

  • The newest and fastest-growing cohort has the highest Israel priority (3.82) — adding Israel to the mission statement will resonate most with SVAJ's growth engine
  • The 7–10 year cohort wants Israel programming (3.69 priority) but is most dissatisfied with what exists (2.91 satisfaction) — this is the implementation gap to close
  • The 5–7 year outlier (3.13) is real but small (n=24) and shouldn't drive the overall strategy — their caution is about politicization, not about Israel itself
  • Legacy members (3.33) are the least likely to prioritize Israel programming — but score Israel as central to values at 4.42 — the issue is programming investment, not identity

So What

What the Tenure Data Means Strategically

The Most Important Finding: The 7–10 Year Cohort Is the Canary

This group — who joined 2015–2017 at the beginning of the Rav Ari era — has the lowest composite satisfaction, lowest Israel programming satisfaction, most financial anxiety, and sharpest youth programming critique of any tenure group. They are also the most engaged (highest Shabbat attendance) and the most ideologically aligned (second-highest mission statement score). They care the most and are the most disappointed. If this cohort starts to disengage or leave, it signals something deeply wrong. If they become the next generation of board leadership — which the data suggests they should be — they will drive the right priorities.

Three Patterns That Cut Across All Cohorts

The welcome is working. The belonging isn't.

Welcoming scores are flat across all tenures (4.12–4.21). Integration scores drop steadily from legacy (4.22) to 2–5 year members (3.86). SVAJ gets people in the door warmly — but deep belonging takes years to build and is clearly not happening fast enough.

Youth programming dissatisfaction grows with exposure.

Legacy members (2.36) are more dissatisfied than newest members (2.91). They've watched the problem build for a decade. This is not a new-member problem — it's a structural failure that compounds over time.

SVAJ is becoming more Zionist as it grows.

Israel priority rises cleanly from 3.33 (legacy) to 3.82 (newest). The community is self-selecting for Zionist identity. The mission statement change isn't aspirational — it's already describing who is joining.
