Section 01

The Strategic Thesis

Before the data, the argument. The survey confirms an intuition, sharpens it into a framework, and surfaces the tensions that must be actively managed.

Core Strategic Argument

SVAJ's long-term health requires making young families with children the primary beneficiaries of programmatic investment over the next three to five years — because they are the generation who will grow the community, fill the seats, provide future leadership, and raise the next generation of SVAJ Jews. At the same time, the older and legacy membership must be repositioned not as passive recipients of services, but as the active financial, volunteer, and mentorship infrastructure that makes investing in young families possible. These are not competing priorities. They are two sides of one strategy. The community that figures out how to make its legacy members the engine of its future will be the one that endures.

The Young Family Case

Young families (roughly age 24–44 with children at home) are SVAJ's growth engine. They account for the majority of new members since 2020, represent the future of the membership base, and bring with them children who — if well served — become the next generation of SVAJ adults. But they are also the most time-constrained, the most likely to leave if their kids' needs aren't met, and the most sensitive to programming quality. Their satisfaction with youth programming (2.72/4.0) is a warning signal, not a crisis — yet. It becomes a crisis if unaddressed over the next two to three years.

Critically: young families chose SVAJ for the rabbi, the warmth, and the hashkafic fit. They did not primarily choose it for its children's programming — only 16 of 175 respondents cited that as a draw. That means SVAJ has earned their entry but not yet their long-term loyalty. Their children's experience over the next five years will determine whether they stay.

The Legacy Member Case

Members who have been at SVAJ for a decade or more are, by the data, highly satisfied across most dimensions. They come weekly, volunteer consistently, and care deeply about the institution. They are also the cohort most worried about what SVAJ is becoming — they miss the "vatikim," feel the intergenerational texture eroding, and want adult learning, chesed infrastructure, and a deeper Shabbat experience. Their concerns are legitimate, not nostalgic.

But more importantly: they have something young families desperately need. Time. Availability. Institutional memory. And in many cases, financial capacity. A strategy that explicitly asks legacy members to invest in young families — through volunteering as madrichim, mentoring new members, funding youth programming, hosting families for Shabbat meals — converts their SVAJ loyalty into fuel for the institution's future rather than a parallel demand on the same scarce resources.

The Five Strategic Tensions to Navigate

1. Growth vs. Intimacy

SVAJ's welcoming culture is what drew people — and it's the thing most at risk from the scale that culture produces. The solution is structural intimacy: grade cohorts, onboarding programs, volunteer ladders that create multiple centers of belonging at manageable scale.

2. Youth Investment vs. Adult Depth

Young families want youth programming; legacy and older members want adult learning and chesed. These demand different staff, different budgets, different time slots. A sequenced capital strategy that builds youth infrastructure first while maintaining adult programming is necessary — not an "either/or."

3. Inclusion vs. Halachic Identity

The "big tent" is SVAJ's greatest asset and its most contested terrain. Members on the right worry the tent is stretching beyond Orthodoxy; members on the left worry it will contract. Leadership must communicate SVAJ's halachic center clearly and confidently — not defensively.

4. Clergy Dependency vs. Institutional Resilience

Rav Ari is irreplaceable as an individual — but his culture, values, and approach to community can be institutionalized. That work must begin now, not when succession becomes urgent.

5. Financial Capacity vs. Programming Ambition

Financial sustainability is the community's #1 strategic priority (3.92/5). Without intentional investment in fundraising infrastructure, every other priority competes for the same thin resource base. Finance is not one priority among many — it is the enabler of all others.

6. Volunteer Bandwidth vs. Young Family Capacity

Young families cite childcare and schedule conflicts as their #1 and #2 engagement barriers. They want to be more involved — but they can't. Legacy members have more discretionary time. Redistributing volunteer roles toward the available cohort, while designing young-family-friendly micro-volunteering, is the structural fix.

Section 06

8 Key Findings

Finding 01

Youth Programming Has Three Separate Failure Modes — and One Fix

The 2.67/4.0 satisfaction score for children's and youth programming is the lowest of any domain. But the qualitative data reveals this isn't one problem — it's three concurrent failures: (A) structural chaos (unsupervised kids, madrichim acting as passive babysitters, no curriculum); (B) the age gap (virtually nothing engaging for ages 9–17, with teens actively migrating to other shuls); and (C) timing mismatch (toddler programming scheduled during naps, evening events past young children's bedtimes).

The fix is also singular: hire a full-time, dedicated Youth Director with programming expertise — not a clergy person with a side portfolio. Multiple respondents name this specifically. Until this hire is made and resourced, the three failure modes will continue on parallel tracks.

"We need a leader who can organize and manage all of the youth programming and related staff. The programming requires planning (logistical, programmatic, and educational), implementation, and supervision. We need someone who can do all of those things." — Current full member, joined 2015, born 1982
"The feedback I am hearing in the Skokie community is that children (9 and up) are bored at SVAJ groups and prefer going to other shuls. Teens especially are bored and disinterested." — Current full member, joined 2015, born 1981
Finding 02

The Rabbi Is the Institution — and That Is a Structural Vulnerability

107 of 175 respondents cited Rav Ari and/or the clergy as the primary reason they joined — nearly 2.5× the next factor. Several respondents articulate the dependency risk directly. One returned to SVAJ after a 30-year absence specifically because of Rav Ari's arrival. Another names him as "the only reason" they stay.

This is not a criticism. It reflects exceptional rabbinic leadership. But institutional resilience requires translating personal culture into organizational culture: hiring practices, volunteer onboarding, new member integration, and governance structures that carry the community's values independent of any individual. The time to do this is now, not when it becomes urgent.

"Rabbi Hart is the key differentiator. Everything that I like about the synagogue flows directly from his lead... we returned in 2022 because of Rabbi Hart." — Current full member, joined 2022
Finding 03

Financial Sustainability Is #1 — and the Enabling Condition for Everything Else

Financial sustainability is the community's top-rated strategic priority (3.92/5; 71% rated it Essential or Very Important). It scores highest among the mid-era cohort (4.10) — the group that has watched SVAJ grow rapidly and can see the gap between ambition and infrastructure. Physical facility satisfaction (3.09/4.0) is the second-lowest domain score; the front entrance stairs appear as a concrete complaint in over a dozen responses.

The strategic implication: financial investment is not one priority among many. It is the prerequisite for the Youth Director hire, the facility upgrade, the adult learning expansion, and the volunteer infrastructure. Without a dedicated development strategy and fundraising capacity, these plans remain aspirational.

Finding 04

The "Too Big to Feel Small" Paradox Is Real and Accelerating

Multiple respondents articulate the same anxiety independently: SVAJ's welcoming culture — the very thing that drew them — is threatened by the scale that culture produces. The Kiddush is "overwhelming." "Everyone has their group." New members fall through the cracks. The social connection gap shows up as a barrier across all segments: 17% of young families, 19% of new members, 14% of seniors, and 16% of those without children all cite it.

The solution is not to limit growth but to institutionalize intimacy at scale: grade-based family cohorts, structured new member integration with assigned "connectors," small-group Shabbat learning pods, volunteer roles that create natural relationship-building. The community needs multiple smaller centers of belonging within the larger tent.

"Growing too large to really feel like a community. Large shuls become hard to navigate socially. Everyone has their 'group' and stops welcoming new friendships." — Current full member
Finding 05

Israel Is a Mission-Level Decision Waiting to Happen

The most consistent single theme across free-text mission statement feedback is the call to add explicit Israel/Zionist language. Connection to Israel rates 4.42/5 in values centrality — statistically tied for third with davening and halachic commitment. Yet the current mission statement contains no mention of Israel, a fact that dozens of respondents note with varying degrees of urgency.

This is a values alignment issue as much as a programming issue. Seniors rate Israel programming as their highest-priority area (3.73); young adults under 40 also rate it at 3.61. The middle cohort (40–54) is the outlier at 3.27 — the most politically diverse group, most likely navigating professional environments where Israel is contested. Adding Israel to the mission statement is broadly supported but requires careful language that affirms Zionism without weaponizing it politically.

"Stay big tent — for Zionists. This is a Zionist congregation full stop, and if you do not fully support that, it is not the place for you." — Current full member, joined 2022, born 1992
Finding 06

Adult Learning Is Underserved for Everyone Under 55

Only 3% of respondents attend shiurim weekly. 32% rarely or never engage with adult learning. Yet adult education rates 3.66/5 in strategic importance overall — and 4.14/5 among members 55+. The disconnect: current offerings appear to serve the long-tenured, older member reasonably well (3.34/4 satisfaction for seniors) but have not found their footing with under-40s (2.87/4) or new members (3.08/4).

The demand is latent and real. Several respondents specifically name weeknight chevrutah learning, women's Torah study tracks, and Israel-focused education as formats that would draw them. The question is not whether to invest in adult learning — it is how to design it so the under-40 and newer-member cohorts actually show up.

Finding 07

Women Are Carrying the Operational Load — and Signaling Where It's Breaking

Women rate almost every strategic priority higher than men. The largest gender gaps: intergenerational connection (+0.59), formalizing programming and planning transparency (+0.39), supporting wellness (+0.37), chesed infrastructure (+0.29), and leadership pipeline (+0.26). The only items men rate higher are financial sustainability (marginally) and membership growth (marginally).

The planning transparency gap is particularly diagnostic: women rate it 3.70 vs. men's 3.31. Women are disproportionately the ones managing family schedules, researching what's available, and getting burned by inconsistent communication about youth programming timing. This is simultaneously a gender insight and a UX insight: communications systems need to be designed for the person managing the family calendar, not the person who shows up to daven.

Additionally, 63 of 175 respondents (37%) want expanded roles for women in ritual life and leadership. Women's participation is a stated differentiator — "as an observant woman I can still breathe here" is a recurring theme. Programming and governance should reflect this explicitly.

Finding 08

The Senior and Single Adult Populations Are Being Overlooked — at a Cost

Older adults and seniors are the most frequently cited group when respondents are asked who SVAJ could do better at including. The honorary member who reported acoustics problems, difficulty viewing the Torah reading, and stairs too steep to navigate — these are not edge cases. They represent a structural failure to serve the founding generation with the same intentionality extended to young families.

Similarly, single adults — divorced, widowed, never married — appear in the data as an underfunded segment. One member's response captures it precisely: "I often feel uncomfortable among the Orthodox community and their obsession with marriage. I feel less that way at SVAJ." Less uncomfortable is not the same as well-served. The community's family-forward programming naturally underserves those outside that structure, and the survey reveals the cost in belonging scores and low engagement.

"Sadly in the last 10 years the connection we had to legacy members is not the same — too many are no longer able to come to shul, or have passed away. This was a very special multi-generational space. Now we are missing the 'vatikim.'" — Current full member, joined 2017, born 1964

Reading the Heatmap: The Three Structural Insights

Insight 1: Youth Programming Is Uniquely Young-Family Driven

The 4.25–4.26 score among young families and under-40s vs. 3.51 among seniors represents the largest demographic split of any priority (0.74 gap). This is the clearest mandate in the dataset: young families are demanding this investment, and no other cohort is blocking it at meaningful rates.

Insight 2: Adult Learning Is a Seniors' Priority Universally Underdelivered

Seniors rate adult learning at 4.14/5 — tied with financial sustainability as their #1 ask. But even new members (3.60) and females across age groups (3.67) want more of it. The deficit is in format and timing, not desire. This is a win-win investment if designed right.

Insight 3: Intergenerational Connection Has an Asymmetric Constituency

Legacy members rate it 3.96 — nearly their #1 priority. Young families rate it 2.98 — dead last. This asymmetry is a design challenge: the people who most want intergenerational connection are not the people young families naturally seek it with. Programming must create the conditions for this encounter, not assume it will happen organically.

Section 09

3-Year Action Roadmap

Actions are sequenced by urgency and enabling dependencies. Year 1 focuses on the youth crisis and financial foundation. Year 2 builds the intergenerational infrastructure. Year 3 deepens and expands.

Y1

Year 1 — Stop the Bleeding, Build the Foundation Most Urgent

  • Youth Director hire — full-time, dedicated, not clergy-adjacent. This is the most important hire of the next 12 months. Budget it first.
  • Shabbat groups overhaul — age-segmented groups with curriculum, safety protocols, trained madrichim, and attendance accountability. Define expectations for children being in groups or supervised by parents.
  • Tot Shabbat restructure — split 0–2 and 3–5 cohorts. Fix timing (nothing during nap time). Consistent snacks. Larger dedicated space.
  • Financial sustainability campaign launch — capital campaign feasibility study, prospective donor identification, professional development capacity assessment.
  • Front entrance accessibility project — scoping, design, funding. High visibility, broad constituency, bounded scope. Lead the capital narrative with this.
  • New member integration program — assigned "connectors," 90-day check-ins, cohort welcome dinners. Reduce the 17–19% social connection gap among new members.
  • Senior volunteer recruitment — structured program to match available senior members with Shabbat group supervision and new family hosting roles. Create the intergenerational connection seniors want while relieving the young family logistics burden.
Y2

Year 2 — Build the Intergenerational Bridge Structural Investment

  • Teen programming track — Rav Ari "Ask Me Anything" sessions, teen-led davening opportunities, spiritual Q&A, hashkafa discussions. Differentiate from day school programming. Serve the public school kid specifically.
  • Grade cohort social program — organized grade-by-grade family social events (park meetups, Shabbat dinners) to build peer community within the school-age groups. Formalize the organic networking that already happens.
  • Adult learning redesign — weeknight chevrutah pairs, women's Torah study track, Israel-focused education series. Design for the under-40 and new member cohort, not only the existing learner base.
  • Lay leadership pipeline — formal committee onboarding, board cultivation program, explicit invitation to mid-era and new members to take on governance roles.
  • Israel identity statement — add Israel/Zionist language to the mission statement. Engage the community in the language-crafting process. Develop an annual Israel programming calendar.
  • Social hall renovation scoping — assess rental opportunity, accessibility improvements, and space optimization for growing event demand.
  • Mission statement refresh — incorporate Israel language, simplify "ahavat achim v'achayot," add language about spiritual growth. Engage broad community in co-creation.
Y3

Year 3 — Deepen, Expand, and Institutionalize Consolidation & Growth

  • Female clergy hire (Maharat/Rabbanit) — multiple respondents name this as a significant opportunity. Assess feasibility, community appetite, and budget capacity. 63% of respondents want expanded roles for women.
  • Davening experience enhancement — informed by what new members and legacy members each want. May include Hashkama minyan option, pace adjustments, or enhanced musical elements.
  • Chesed infrastructure formalization — structured volunteer program, community support for members in crisis (divorce, illness, loss), food pantry expansion.
  • Senior inclusion programming — dedicated accessibility investment (acoustics, seating), intergenerational Shabbat programming, recognition of legacy member contributions.
  • Community positioning — SVAJ as the "premier centrist Orthodox institution in Chicagoland." Engage with peer shuls, Hillel Torah, and the Skokie Jewish community ecosystem from a position of strength.
  • Clergy continuity planning — board-level conversation about succession culture, institutional resilience, and what SVAJ would need to sustain its character through a rabbinic transition.
  • Single adult programming — dedicated social and programming tracks for divorced, widowed, and never-married members across age groups.

Section 10

The Next Level of Questions

The survey has answered the "what" with unusual clarity. The following questions are the "how" — the strategic unknowns that should drive the committee's next phase of work, whether through focus groups, interviews, or supplemental data.

On Youth Programming

  • Q1What do kids aged 9–14 actually want from a Shabbat morning experience? Have we ever asked them directly?
  • Q2What is the full-time Youth Director hire budget, reporting structure, and success criteria? What does Year 1 accountability look like?
  • Q3How many SVAJ families are currently sending their kids to another shul on Shabbat morning? Can we quantify the attrition?
  • Q4Which shuls (KCT, Or Torah, others) are SVAJ kids gravitating to, and what specifically do those programs offer that ours doesn't?
  • Q5Can public school families be better served? Several responses note that groups skew day-school. Is there a dedicated public school track that would differentiate SVAJ?

On the Intergenerational Strategy

  • Q6How many senior members are currently available and willing to volunteer in structured roles? Has anyone inventoried this?
  • Q7What would a "grade cohort" social model look like structurally? Who runs it, how is it funded, how does it scale as new grades age in?
  • Q8What does "recognition of legacy members" look like in practice? Is there a formal program, a naming convention, a public acknowledgment structure?

On Financial Sustainability

  • Q9What is the actual 5-year capital needs number? Building assessment, accessibility, social hall, elevator, staffing growth — what does full investment cost?
  • Q10What is the social hall rental revenue opportunity? Has it been modeled? What renovation would make it rentable at meaningful scale?
  • Q11Who are SVAJ's top 20 prospective major donors? What is the current relationship with each of them?
  • Q12What is the ROI threshold on a professional development officer hire? What are comparable shuls paying and raising?

On Identity and Mission

  • Q13What exact language for Israel/Zionism in the mission statement would pass a community vote? Pilot 2–3 options with focus groups before committing.
  • Q14What is the board's position on the Kol Sasson relationship? Is there a written agreement, and what are the terms of the arrangement?
  • Q15What does SVAJ's halachic "floor and ceiling" look like as a written, communicable statement? This would reduce the left-right tension significantly.

On Scale and Growth

  • Q16What is SVAJ's capacity ceiling — at what household count does the current building, staff model, and culture break?
  • Q17Is a second location (Skokie Valley West, satellite minyan) a 5-year scenario? What triggers that decision?
  • Q18What does the formal new member onboarding look like today, and where does it fail? Is there a 90-day check-in? An assigned connector?
  • Q19What does Rav Ari's sustainable workload look like? Is there a board-level conversation about supporting his capacity and planning for succession culture?
Closing Thought

"Right now, Skokie is looking to SVAJ as the voice of our Jewish community. It's ours to lose." The survey data confirms this — the reputation, the growth momentum, the talent, the goodwill are all there. What remains is the institutional will to invest intentionally, to ask the older generation to actively support the younger one, and to build the infrastructure that will make SVAJ's culture outlast any single leader or era. That is the work of the next three years.
