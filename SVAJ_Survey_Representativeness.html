Represent Our Actual Community?

Comparing 175 survey responses against the full 1,215-person membership database (358 households, 620 adults). After deduplication, the survey is broadly representative — with meaningful gaps among seniors, associate members, and families with tweens and teens.

The Strategic Impact of These Gaps

The survey's findings are reliable for the cohorts it captured well — particularly the 30–54 age range and the core family membership. The gaps affect two specific areas of the strategic plan:

  • Senior and associate member needs are likely understated. If 65+ members are underrepresented by 7 points, their priorities (accessibility, adult learning, intergenerational connection) are receiving less weight in the data than their actual share of the membership warrants. The strategic plan's attention to seniors is correct — but may be even more urgent than the survey suggests.
  • The 40–49 cohort's dissatisfaction may be undercounted. This is the group with school-age kids whose youth programming frustration is most acute. A 9-point underrepresentation means that the youth programming satisfaction score of 2.67 might actually be lower if this cohort responded at full rate.

The 40–49 Gap: Why It Matters for Youth Programming

The 40–49 cohort is the group most likely to have school-age children (ages 9–14) — the exact demographic at the center of the youth programming crisis. They are also the peak career and childcare years, making them most time-constrained and least likely to complete a long survey. Their absence means the youth programming satisfaction score of 2.67/4.0 may actually be optimistically high. If this cohort responded at full rate, it could be lower. For the next survey, targeted outreach to this cohort — shorter format, mobile-friendly, personal ask from a peer — would improve the data.

Who Are Associate Members?

The database reveals that associate members are a demographically distinct cohort:

  • →Mean age: 58 — significantly older than full member average
  • →39% are 65 or older
  • →95 associate adults total across 62 households
  • →Mostly married (65/95), but higher widowed and single rates than full members
  • →Likely on financial hardship arrangements or reduced dues

This population — older, often on fixed income, more likely to be widowed or in transition — is precisely the group most reliant on SVAJ's chesed infrastructure, accessibility, and community connection. Their near-absence from the survey means their needs are systematically underweighted in the findings.

The Kol Sasson Overlap

The database identifies 11 households with "KS" (Kol Sasson) in their account type, representing 20 adults. These are dual-affiliation members — belonging to both SVAJ and Kol Sasson in some capacity. Their survey representation is unknown (the survey did not ask about KS affiliation), but given the repeated mentions of the KS relationship as a source of tension, a targeted conversation with this segment could be strategically valuable.

The Tween Dominance Problem

The single largest group of children in SVAJ is tweens ages 9–13 (117 children) — 40% of all under-18 members. This is exactly the age group that survey respondents describe as most underserved: "bored," "choosing other shuls," "nothing for them." Yet their voice is absent from the survey. The data we have on this group comes entirely through parental proxies.

82 households have at least one child under 10. These are the families most affected by Tot Shabbat quality and young groups scheduling. They are also the families most likely to be in the 40–49 cohort — which is underrepresented in the survey.

Section 07

Who We Missed — Specific Underrepresented Groups

Beyond the broad demographic gaps, the database reveals specific community subgroups whose perspectives are likely absent or thin in the survey data.

Adults 65+ — Seniors

Database: 144 adults (28% of valid ages) · Survey: ~21%

7-percentage-point gap. This group's priorities — adult learning, accessibility, intergenerational connection — are confirmed by survey data but likely even more urgent than the numbers show. The 80+ cohort (33 people in database) is almost certainly underrepresented in the survey's 5% 80+ share.

Associate Members — 62 Households

Database: 17% of all households · Survey: ~11%

Mean age 58, 39% are 65+. These are often members on financial hardship arrangements, widowed longtime members, or older singles. Their needs — financial accessibility, chesed, belonging — are the most at-risk of being invisible in this data. A separate targeted outreach to this population would be valuable.

Ages 40–49 — Peak Parenting Cohort

Database: 26% of adults · Survey: 17% · Gap: −9pts

These are the parents of tweens (9–13), the group most vocal about youth programming failure in the free-text responses. Their underrepresentation suggests the youth programming crisis findings are if anything understated. Highest household demand for programming; lowest survey participation.

Widowed Members — 35 Adults

Database: 35 widowed adults (6% of adults) · Survey: undercounted

Widowed members skew older and female. They are disproportionately in the associate member category (4 of 35 widowed). Their social isolation risk is high; their needs for community connection, chesed, and belonging are distinct from coupled members. The survey had very few responses identifiable as widowed.

Newest Members — 2023 and Later

Database: 61 households · Survey: 24 (39% response rate — lowest)

The lowest response rate of any tenure group. These members have the least context to evaluate the shul and the most to gain from a good integration experience. Their top barriers — "don't know what's available" — suggest the communication gap makes it harder for them to even engage with a survey about SVAJ's future.

Young Adults 18–29 — Only 11 in Database

Database: 11 adults under 30 · Survey: ~5

SVAJ has almost no young single adult membership (18–29). Only 11 in the entire database. This is itself a strategic data point — the community is not attracting young singles or post-college adults as standalone members. The survey's finding that singles feel underserved reflects this structural reality.

Children & Teens — 294 Total

0 directly surveyed · All data from parental proxies

117 tweens, 67 teens — the most underserved programming demographic — have no direct voice in this data. A youth focus group or brief teen survey would be the highest-value supplemental research the committee could commission.

Kol Sasson Dual Members — 11 Households

Database: 11 KS-affiliated accounts · Survey: unknown

The KS relationship surfaces repeatedly as a source of tension and strategic complexity. Understanding how dual-affiliated members experience both communities — and what they want from the relationship — would inform the board's decision about how to structure the arrangement going forward.

What This Means for the Strategic Plan

What the Survey Data Can Be Trusted For

  • ✓Tenure-based analysis — near-perfect representativeness
  • ✓Full member priorities and satisfaction
  • ✓The 30–54 age cohort — well represented
  • ✓Household-level perspectives on youth programming
  • ✓Israel values and identity — strong consensus across all represented groups
  • ✓Strategic priority rankings — reliable directionally

Where to Supplement Before Final Decisions

  • →Associate member needs: 1-1 conversations or a targeted short survey
  • →Teen and tween voice: a youth focus group before hiring the Youth Director
  • →40–49 cohort: targeted outreach in the next survey cycle
  • →Senior members 80+: pastoral visits or small group conversations
  • →Kol Sasson dual members: board-level direct conversation

Overall Verdict: Reliable, With Known Gaps — Act on What You Have

  • At ~49% household response rate, this is one of the stronger congregational survey datasets you will encounter. The strategic plan's core findings are well-supported.
  • The gaps — seniors, associates, 40–49 year olds, tweens/teens — all skew in the same direction: toward understating the urgency of accessibility, chesed, youth programming, and senior inclusion. The strategic plan's priorities are likely understated, not overstated.
  • The next survey (scheduled in the plan for 2027) should be designed with these gaps in mind: shorter format, personal outreach to associate members, mobile-first design, and a separate teen component.
  • The database itself needs modest cleanup: 12 adults with no gender recorded, some adults with birth years that appear to be join dates, and a 1917 join date that is clearly erroneous.
