What makes SVAJ distinct

The open-ended distinctiveness question drew ~70 responses and is strikingly consistent. The dominant theme across the board: inclusive, non-judgmental, welcoming — within a halachic framework. Members describe SVAJ as a "big tent" Modern Orthodox community where people aren't defined by their hat, observance level, economic status, or background.

Specific things members highlight: the parking lot doesn't judge you, women are genuinely integrated (not an afterthought), the culture flows directly from the rabbinic team's tone, and members actively introduce themselves to newcomers. One member: "SVAJ is the only community I've found with commitment to both halachic integrity and genuine inclusion at its core."

41.5% want expanded women's roles — this is not a fringe view. Open-ended requests include a full-time female clergy member present locally, expanded women-led halachic Torah learning, and more ritual opportunities.
At least one member flags the opposite concern: that certain practices at Neilah are moving past what's halachically appropriate and creating a "slippery slope" risk. This is SVAJ's most active and sensitive internal tension.

Biggest opportunities and risks

Top opportunity themes (open-ended)

Youth & teen engagement
Most common opportunity cited. Structured tween/teen programs, integration into main shul, compelling spiritual experiences before college.
Women in clergy/leadership
Multiple calls for a full-time, locally present female clergy member. Also: expanded women-led halachic learning.
National model for centrist Orthodoxy
"Become the standard bearer of inclusive centrist Orthodox Judaism nationally" — named explicitly by multiple respondents.
Singles & young adults
Multiple calls for programming connecting young singles, post-college adults, widows, and divorced members.

Top risk themes (open-ended)

Growth eroding community warmth
Most frequently cited risk. Losing the tight-knit feel, people slipping through the cracks, sanctuary/kiddush crowding, rabbis stretched too thin.
Rabbi dependency
One vision response literally reads: "1. Rav 2. Ari 3. will 4. still 5. be 6. here." Clergy turnover is named as a top risk.
Kol Sasson relationship
Multiple members flag the KS arrangement as a source of tension — facility burden, board representation, and divergent member interests.
Hashkafic drift or identity loss
Some fear moving too far left halachically; others fear moving right. "Cannot be all things to all congregants" appears multiple times.
Physical security
Antisemitism and external threats cited repeatedly. Multiple members specifically mention the fence and security protocols — acknowledging the necessity while noting the cost to welcoming aesthetics.
Financial sustainability
Economic pressure on Orthodox families, fundraising capacity, and the cost of programming growth all flagged. Top strategic priority in the quantitative data.

Groups that could be better included

28% of respondents said SVAJ could do more for specific populations. The most frequently cited groups: older adults and seniors (mentioned in ~10 responses, including one account of a senior who has never been invited to a meal), newcomers to the community, young singles, people with disabilities (accessibility and staircase), and individuals navigating personal challenges (divorce, loss, illness).

23 of 83 respondents (28%) said "No — Skokie Valley is welcoming to all." That's a meaningful baseline of satisfaction, but it means 72% either identified specific gaps or weren't sure.

Strategic implications for the planning committee

1

Fix youth programming — structurally, not incrementally

This is the clearest, most data-backed gap. 36% dissatisfied, lowest score of any area. Needs dedicated investment in structure, staffing, and teen integration into main shul life — not minor tweaks.

2

Develop a deliberate growth management strategy

The single most common risk theme is that growth is eroding the warmth that makes SVAJ distinctive. Newcomer integration, intentional social mixing, and space/kiddush design all need strategic attention.

3

Reduce single points of failure in clergy and leadership

Rabbi-dependency is explicit in the survey — multiple respondents named clergy turnover as the top risk. Lay leadership development and volunteer pipeline investment are the lever here.

4

Address the physical plant

Staircase accessibility, social hall capacity, and kitchen upgrades appear enough across open-ended responses to warrant capital planning. Financial sustainability is the top-ranked strategic priority — these likely connect.

5

Make proactive decisions on women's roles and KS relationship

Neither issue will resolve on its own. Both deserve values-grounded, intentional decision-making — the survey shows clear community interest in more clarity, not continued drift.

6

Double down on the welcoming culture — it's the brand

"Welcoming community" is the highest-rated value (4.77) and appears in vision words, distinctiveness descriptions, and attraction factors more than any other theme. Any strategic decision should be filtered through its effect on this.
